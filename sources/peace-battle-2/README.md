# Peace-Battle 2 — source material

The game in `apps/peace-battle-2/` is a knock-off of *The Landlord's Game*, which Lizzie
Magie invented and which became Monopoly. This folder holds what the game's claims rest on.

Not precached by the service worker, not part of the offline download.

## The rules

`landlords-game-and-prosperity-1932-rules.pdf` — the printed rules sheet of *The Landlord's
Game and Prosperity*, copyright 1932 by Adgame Co., Inc., 1735 Fourteenth Street N.W.,
Washington, D.C. Eight pages: implements, board spaces, setup, then fourteen numbered rules
for the Landlord's Game and eleven for Prosperity.
Online: https://landlordsgame.info/games/lgp-1932/lgp-1932_rules.pdf

Everything the game does mechanically comes from it:

- Landlord's Game, opening paragraph and Rule 13: the object is to become the Big Landlord,
  won on accumulating $2,000. It is not won by outlasting the others.
- Rule 12: a player who runs short sells cards to meet the obligation. Going broke is a
  transaction, not an elimination — and such a player may combine with two others to
  establish Prosperity Rules.
- Rule 14: the players can change from Landlord's Game to Prosperity Rules at any time
  during the game, by agreement.
- Rule 1(a): land rent is doubled on every green space on a side of the board once four or
  more Improvements stand on that side. Optional A: Improvements cost $250 and earn $100
  rent on top of the land rent.
- Rule 11: wages at Your Wages Corner scale with the throw — double 6 pays $800 (expert
  specialist), double 5 pays $700 (professional), double 4, 3 or 2 pays $600 (manager),
  any other exact count pays $500 (clerk), and arriving other than by exact count pays
  day-laborer's wages.
- Rules 5, 6 and 7: personal property tax, land tax, improvements tax, all at blue Tax
  Office spaces. Rule 8: Lord Blueblood's Estate is trespassing, go back to Jail. Rule 9:
  Jail costs a $50 fine and six or more spots to leave.
- Prosperity Rule 1: all taxes abolished; land rents paid into the Prosperity Land Rent
  Fund; the landlord retains only the rent on the Improvement.
- Prosperity Rules 2 and 3: the fund condemns and buys the local utilities at $200 each,
  then the railroads at $300, and their charges drop once public.
- Prosperity Rules 8 and 9: Lord Blueblood's Estate becomes a public park with no charge,
  and there is no longer any Jail.
- Prosperity Rule 11: "When the player with the least money has $7000 (double his original
  capital), Prosperity is achieved."
- The sheet's own summary: "The Landlord's Game shows why our national housekeeping has
  gone wrong and Prosperity Game shows how to start it right and keep it going right."

Two numbers in the sheet disagree with each other. The opening paragraph gives every player
$300; the setup table itemizes $350 (2 green at $100, 2 yellow at $50, 4 blue at $10, 2 red
at $5). Prosperity Rule 11 prints the target as $7000, which is double neither. $350 and
$700 are the pair that make the document consistent, so the game uses those and says so on
screen.

## The history

`smithsonian-the-woman-inventor-behind-monopoly.pdf` — "The Woman Inventor Behind
'Monopoly'", Monica M. Smith, Smithsonian Lemelson Center for the Study of Invention and
Innovation, published March 26 2015.
Online: https://invention.si.edu/invention-stories/woman-inventor-behind-monopoly

- Elizabeth "Lizzie" J. Magie Phillips, 1866–1948. Worked as a stenographer and typist at
  the Dead Letter Office. Her first patent, in 1893, was for a typewriter part.
- She took up Henry George's argument that a single land value tax would shift the burden
  onto wealthy landlords, and built a board game to teach it.
- The Landlord's Game, US patent 748,626, granted January 5 1904. Patented again with a
  revised board as US 1,509,312, September 23 1924.
- Her own statement of the game's object, from the 1924 application:

  > the object of the game is not only to afford amusement to the players, but to
  > illustrate to them how under the present or prevailing system of land tenure, the
  > landlord has an advantage over other enterprises and also how the single tax would
  > discourage land speculation.

- Square names from the 1924 board: Poverty Place, Goat Alley, Rickety Row, Hell's Half
  Acre, Easy Street, The Bowery, Lake Shore Drive, Progress Park, George Street, Fels
  Avenue, Soakum Lighting, Johnson Road, Gee-Whiz R.R. and The Public Highway, plus a
  corner reading "Labor Upon Mother Earth Produces Wages", a Poor House and Public Park
  corner, and Lord Blueblood's estate. The game uses these names.
- She sold her patent rights to Parker Brothers in November 1935 for $500 and no
  royalties. Charles Darrow patented Monopoly the month after, US 2,026,082, December 1935,
  and Parker Brothers sold it with Darrow as the sole inventor. She died in obscurity in
  1948.
- From the Evening Star, January 28 1936, on how she took it:

  > if the subtle propaganda for the single tax idea works around to the minds of the
  > thousands who now shake the dice and buy and sell over the "Monopoly" board, she feels
  > the whole business will not have been in vain.

## What is the game's own and not hers

The 1932 board has forty spaces; this one has twenty, to fit a phone. The shortening forces
everything in this list, and the game states all of it on screen:

- One local utility and one railroad, where the sheet has three of each, so the monopoly
  rates in Rule 2(d) and Rule 3(e) cannot arise.
- One Tax Office doing the work of the sheet's three blue offices, charged together.
- Rule 1(a)'s doubling threshold lowered from four Improvements per side to two, because a
  side here carries two or three buildable squares rather than four or five.
- Day-laborer's wages set at $100. Rule 11 names the other five rates and not this one.
- Starting capital $350, per the setup table rather than the opening paragraph.
- Real Estate Offices, Idle Land, the Gold Mine, no-trespassing signs, bidding, and player
  to player trade are all in the sheet and not in the game.

## What is deliberately not claimed

That Magie patented two rule sets. She did not; the patents describe one game. The two rule
sets are the 1932 edition's, published seven years before her death and three years before
she sold the patent.
