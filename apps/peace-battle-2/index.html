How to play

The bar at the top is your day. Nineteen segments are shadowed. One glows. That is your five percent. Tap it, then tap a card on the map to spend it there. Once a day, no more.

The cards sit over hearths. Each one is a goal with a finish line and a few tasks small enough for half an hour. A task needs slivers from two to four different hearths. Yours counts as one. The rest arrive over the following days from other lit hearths, as small lights along the roads. When every task on a card is done, the goal is reached, the hearth lights, its trade's skills join the supply bar, and a new card appears somewhere else.

Spending your sliver lights a road from your hearth to that one. Fog cannot cross a lit road.

Post a goal puts a card on your own hearth. Other hearths finish it. When it is reached, your hearth burns brighter and your sector's supply moves up.

Some days the fog sits on your hearth and the attacks take the day. The segment goes gray. Nothing you had put on a task is lost. The day passes.

From day ten, your own hearth offers a lamp: Take it further. It names a first customer, lights one more road a day on its own, and supplies a sector skill every few days. It is optional. The game is complete without it.

When every hearth in a land is lit, the land sings. The fog is pushed out and cannot come back. A road between two singing lands joins them. The country is the set of singing lands.

You win when the country supplies 99 percent of what it needs. Fifty years are on the ring around the map, seven days each. If they run out first, the run ends there.

What it is a picture of

The five percent

Most of a Targeted Individual's day goes to the attacks and to what they force. What is left might be five percent. Twenty members with five percent of a day each is a full working day.

The goal board

The cards are the goal board in PeerProgramming. Post one personal goal with a finish line. Break it into tasks small enough to do from a phone in under half an hour. Take one card from anybody else's goal. A bad day costs nothing.

384

The marker on the exchanges bar is 384: members exchanging with each other on one day, counted a day at a time. It is reached when the typical day across a year sits at or above 384, not when one day touches it.

The supply bar

Peace Battle 2 is finished when a survivor can get 99 percent of what they need, housing, transportation, goods and services, from other survivors. Not 100 percent. The gray end of the bar is a phone and a vehicle.

One Percent

The lamp is One Percent, the paid tier: a half hour in your browser on the figure your trade produces and the first customer behind it. Self-service is complete without it.

The singing lands

Estonia had 1.3 million people. It was occupied by a power that outnumbered them by more than a hundred to one and surveilled them through an informant network that reached into every town. They kept their language, kept their identity, and took their country back. The singing lands are named for that.

Why it is distributed

Targeting is spread across every country, race, class and religion, so the answer is distributed too. A protest in a place assumes neighbors, money for travel and a day that can be given up. Most people this is for have none of the three.

The Du Bois line

In 1903 W. E. B. Du Bois looked back at what the founders of the colleges had done after the Civil War: 2,000 trained, who trained 50,000, who taught nine millions. He called it the most wonderful peace-battle of the 19th century. This is the second one.

Where the numbers come from

The day-one map is the Skills Economy's own figures on the day the game was built: 174 people on the skills map, 230 of 713 skills somebody can be found for, a five-million survivor population. The lit hearths on day one are spread across sectors the way the Directory is. Each hearth stands for many people. Nothing here is a prediction.
